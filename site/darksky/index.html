Method

Night lights. NASA's Black Marble VNP46A2 daily radiance (gap-filled and corrected for moonlight), via the GIBS imagery service. I take the median of 7 dates per year on a ~1 km grid for 2014 and 2025. GIBS serves an 8-bit display stretch, so values are a radiance index rather than calibrated units, and bright city cores saturate.

Ground truth. Globe at Night Sky Quality Meter readings from 2014–2025 across western North America. Only clear skies with the sun at least 15° below the horizon are used. Observation times are rebuilt from local time, because the published UT field has a sign error in the 2014–2022 releases.

Two models. A median model predicts a typical reading. An 80th-percentile model predicts a good night: readings are only ever contaminated brighter (haze, twilight, stray light), so the upper quantile is the better estimate of the sky's real darkness. The maps and site scores use the good-night model.

Night score. For each hour of astronomical darkness I multiply clear-sky fraction by moonlight penalty, then by the site's sky quality, and average across the night.

Honest limitations

  • The model explains about half the variation in individual readings. Globe at Night readings are noisy: observer technique, haze and local lights all add scatter.
  • Bright city cores saturate the night-light index, so the model can't tell a busy downtown from a suburb. Brightening is undercounted where lights were already saturated in 2014.
  • Snow, desert reflectance and the satellite's 1:30 am overpass all affect the index. Changes in LED colour (blue-rich light that VIIRS undercounts) mean real brightening is probably larger than shown.
  • Training data is thin in BC: fewer than 500 Canadian readings. BC predictions lean on patterns learned in Washington, Oregon and California.
